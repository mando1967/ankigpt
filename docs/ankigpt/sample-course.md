# Introduction to Microeconomics — Lecture Notes (Weeks 1–3)

## 1. Scarcity and opportunity cost

Economics studies how people make choices under scarcity: wants are unlimited, resources are not. Every choice therefore has an opportunity cost — the value of the next best alternative that is given up. The opportunity cost of attending a lecture is not the tuition fee (already paid) but the best thing you could have done with those two hours.

Opportunity cost is not only monetary. A key habit of economic reasoning is to ask "compared to what?" whenever a decision is evaluated.

## 2. The production possibilities frontier

The production possibilities frontier (PPF) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology. Points on the frontier are efficient; points inside it are inefficient (unemployed or misallocated resources); points outside it are unattainable for now.

The PPF is usually bowed outward because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods. As more of one good is produced, resources that are progressively less suited to it must be diverted, so the opportunity cost of each additional unit rises. This is the law of increasing opportunity cost.

Economic growth — more resources or better technology — shifts the frontier outward.

## 3. Comparative advantage and gains from trade

A producer has an absolute advantage if it can make a good using fewer inputs. It has a comparative advantage if it can make the good at a lower opportunity cost. Trade is mutually beneficial when each party specialises in the good in which it has a comparative advantage, even if one party has an absolute advantage in everything.

Example: if Ana can make 4 shirts or 2 loaves per hour and Ben can make 1 shirt or 1 loaf, Ana has an absolute advantage in both. But Ana's opportunity cost of a loaf is 2 shirts, Ben's is 1 shirt, so Ben has the comparative advantage in bread. Both gain if Ben bakes and Ana sews, trading at a price between their opportunity costs.

## 4. Demand

The demand curve shows the quantity of a good buyers are willing and able to purchase at each price, holding everything else constant (ceteris paribus). It slopes downward: at a lower price, the good is cheaper relative to substitutes (substitution effect) and buyers' real income is higher (income effect).

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve. A change in anything else — income, prices of substitutes or complements, tastes, expectations, the number of buyers — shifts the whole curve. For a normal good, higher income shifts demand right; for an inferior good, it shifts demand left.

## 5. Supply

The supply curve shows the quantity sellers are willing to offer at each price. It slopes upward because higher prices cover the rising marginal cost of producing additional units and attract new sellers. Input prices, technology, expectations, and the number of sellers shift supply; the good's own price moves along the curve.

## 6. Market equilibrium

Equilibrium is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Above it there is a surplus, and competition among sellers pushes the price down; below it there is a shortage, and competition among buyers pushes the price up. Markets therefore tend toward equilibrium.

To predict the effect of an event, use three steps: decide whether it shifts supply or demand, decide the direction of the shift, and compare the old and new equilibria. When both curves shift, the change in either price or quantity is ambiguous without knowing the relative sizes of the shifts.

## 7. Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a price change: the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic when the ratio exceeds 1 and inelastic when it is below 1. Goods with close substitutes, luxuries, and broadly defined markets over long time horizons tend to have more elastic demand.

Elasticity determines how total revenue responds to a price change: with inelastic demand, raising the price raises revenue; with elastic demand, raising the price lowers it.
